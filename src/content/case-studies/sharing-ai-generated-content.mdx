I like to discourage a particular practice: generating something with AI and sharing it with others to get their feedback on the AI's output. I think anything you generate with AI should be thoroughly vetted (and edited, if necessary) before you share it.

It happens in a lot of settings (e.g., a draft policy sent to a committee, a lesson plan shared with a colleague, a block of code posted for review, a proposal sent to a supervisor). The details differ, but the problem is the same.

## The awkward position it creates

Sharing unvetted AI output places others in an awkward position. They're asked to evaluate it without the context of the person who prompted the AI. That leaves them with questions they can't answer:

- What are we evaluating? The AI's output, the person's idea behind the prompt, or something else?
- What were the prompts and the context?
- Which ideas are the person's, and which did the AI introduce?
- What's hallucinated?

AI also typically generates much more content than the prompter intended, which leaves the reviewer sifting the wheat from the chaff. Worse, AI often adds unintended fluff, [hallucinations](/ai-and-humans/#it-hallucinates), and bias. The person who prompted it is in the best position to catch those problems. When they skip that step, the work lands on someone else.

Researchers have a name for a closely related problem at work: "workslop," AI-generated work that looks polished but doesn't actually move the task forward. In a [survey of 1,150 U.S. full-time employees](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity), 40% said they'd received workslop in the past month, and they spent nearly two hours, on average, dealing with each instance. About half said they viewed the colleagues who sent it as less capable and less reliable. As the researchers put it, workslop "transfers the effort from creator to receiver."

## What I'd rather see

In these situations, I'd be more interested in content the person created themselves and takes full ownership of, whether or not AI was involved in the creative process.

I view AI-generated content as not inherently trustworthy, traceable, responsible, etc. In contrast, human content (created with or without AI assistance) can be all of those things, because there's a person behind it who can explain it and answer for it.

To a certain extent, this situation is comparable to grading a student submission that's completely AI generated. Whatever I'd be evaluating, it wouldn't be the student's learning.

When I talk with students about what they generate with AI, I shift the focus away from the content itself. Instead, I ask how they stewarded the AI to create something that solves a problem meaningful to them, and how they made sure the AI's output matched their vision and intention for the solution.

There's one exception worth naming. Sometimes the AI's output really is what you want feedback on (e.g., you're comparing AI systems, or testing whether a prompt works). That's fine, as long as you say so plainly and share your prompts and context along with the output.

## Applying the Faithful Automation framework

Here's how I answer the four questions of the [Faithful Automation framework](/framework/) for sharing AI-generated content.

### Intent

**What is my task, its purpose, and my intent in using AI to assist in completing it or to automate it entirely?**

The task is to create something worth another person's time, and the purpose of asking for feedback is to make it better. If I share raw AI output, my intent is arguably to skip the part of the work that's mine: deciding what I actually think and what I want to say.

Note that asking for feedback is a request for someone else's time and attention. That request deserves my best effort, and AI's first draft isn't my best effort.

### Truth

**How am I anchoring truth, knowing AI is neither transparent nor inherently trustworthy?**

Vetting is how I anchor truth before anything leaves my hands. That means reading every word and checking every fact and citation. Anything I can't verify, or wouldn't have said myself, gets cut.

If I share unvetted output, I'm asking someone else to anchor truth for me, without the context they'd need to do it well.

### Stewardship

**How am I stewarding my own thinking, my time, and my gifts, recognizing the formative nature of this task?**

Editing AI output into something I'd put my name on is where my own thinking happens. It forces me to decide what I mean.

Skipping that step might save me time, but it spends the reviewer's time instead (nearly two hours per instance, in the workslop survey), and it costs me the formation that comes from doing the work. It's the same reason I ask students how they stewarded AI rather than focusing on what it produced.

### Connection

**How is my use of AI displacing human connection that would otherwise occur when completing this task?**

Asking for feedback is relational. When I ask a colleague for feedback, I'm inviting them into my thinking. If what I share is mostly the AI's thinking, the conversation that follows is between my colleague and the AI, with me [in between](/framework/#4-connection).

It can also cost trust. In the workslop survey, 42% of respondents saw colleagues who sent workslop as less trustworthy.

## Before you share

- Read every word, and cut what you wouldn't have written yourself.
- Verify the facts, numbers, quotes, and citations.
- Make sure you can explain and defend all of it without AI's help.
- If AI significantly shaped the ideas, say so.
- If you do want feedback on the AI's output itself, say that plainly, and include your prompts and context.
