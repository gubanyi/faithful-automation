AI is an [ultra-intelligent black box](/what-is-ai/#an-ultra-intelligent-black-box), and both AI and the people who use it have [predictable tendencies](/ai-and-humans/) that can lead us astray. So how do you decide whether, and how, to use it?

Faithful Automation doesn't hand you a list of rules. It gives you six questions, organized around the natural arc of any task: how you **approach** the task, how you **engage** with AI while you work, and how you **reflect** afterward. Together, they put a simple motto into practice: *faithful to the truth, honest with yourself and others.*

The questions scale with the stakes. A quick, low-stakes question to a chatbot might take a few seconds of thought. Handing AI work that affects your learning, your job, or other people deserves more. With practice, the questions become habits.

## Approach

*Before you begin, get clear about the task, and about yourself.*

### 1. Purpose

**What task am I completing, and what is its purpose?**

Every task has a purpose beyond getting it done. Sometimes the point is the **product**: a clear email, an accurate report, working code. Sometimes the point is the **process**: learning a skill, practicing a craft, working through a hard problem, or showing someone you care. Often it's both. The purpose tells you what's at stake when you hand part of the work to AI.

- Who is this for, and what do they actually need?
- Is the value in the finished product, in the doing, or both?
- What would be lost if something else did this for me?

### 2. Intent

**What is my intent in using AI for this task?**

Be honest with yourself about why you're reaching for AI. Some reasons are healthy: saving time on tedious work, getting unstuck, catching mistakes, exploring ideas. Others deserve a second look: avoiding effort you actually need to invest, or appearing more knowledgeable than you are.

Intent also includes honesty with others. If the people affected by your work would be surprised or disappointed to learn how you used AI, pay attention to that.

- Am I trying to do this task *better*, or trying to *avoid* it?
- Would I be comfortable explaining how I used AI to the people this work affects?
- Is AI the right tool here, or just the most convenient one?

## Engage

*While you work, stay grounded in reality and keep your own mind in the game.*

### 3. Truth

**How am I anchoring truth, knowing AI is neither transparent nor fully trustworthy?**

AI [hallucinates, sounds confident when it's wrong, and tends to agree with you](/ai-and-humans/#how-ai-tends-to-behave), and you can't see where its knowledge comes from. Anchoring truth means treating AI output as a draft to check, not a verdict to accept. This matters most when you're outside your expertise, because that is when errors and flattery are hardest to spot.

- **Verify what matters.** Check important facts, numbers, quotes, and citations against reliable sources, and actually open those sources.
- **Be wary of easy agreement.** Ask your question without revealing your opinion, or ask AI to make the strongest case against your view.
- **Match your checking to the stakes.** A brainstorm needs little verification. A medical, legal, financial, or academic decision needs a lot, often including a knowledgeable person.
- **Know your limits.** If you couldn't recognize a wrong answer, don't rely on AI alone.

### 4. Thinking

**How am I stewarding my own thinking, knowing that tasks shape who I become?**

The work we do forms us. Writing clarifies our thinking, wrestling with problems builds skill, and making decisions develops judgment. When AI does that work for us, we may gain speed but [lose the growth](/ai-and-humans/#we-prefer-instant-answers) the work would have produced. Stewarding your thinking means deciding deliberately which thinking to keep and which to hand off.

- **Think first.** Sketch your own ideas, outline, or approach before asking AI.
- **Use AI as a coach, not a ghostwriter.** Ask for hints, questions, and feedback rather than finished answers, especially while you're learning.
- **Protect the skills that matter.** Keep practicing the abilities your work, studies, and life depend on.
- **Own the result.** Make sure you can explain, defend, and take responsibility for anything you use, without AI's help.

## Reflect

*Afterward, look at what AI did for your relationships and your work.*

### 5. Relationships

**Did AI replace human connections that strengthen my relationships?**

Many tasks are also chances to connect: asking a colleague for help, talking a problem through with a friend, getting feedback from a teacher, or mentoring someone newer. AI is always available, endlessly patient, and never judgmental, which makes it easy to skip the people. But those small interactions build trust and belonging that no tool can replace.

- Did I ask AI something I would once have asked a person? What did we both miss?
- Did AI help me serve and connect with others, or did it put distance between us?
- Was I honest with others about how I used AI?

### 6. Joy

**How did AI affect the joy I find in my work?**

Work, whether it's a job, a class, a creative project, or caring for others, is more than output. Much of its meaning comes from craft, curiosity, creativity, and the satisfaction of doing something well. Used wisely, AI can clear away drudgery and leave more room for the parts you love. Used carelessly, it can take away the very parts that make the work meaningful.

- Did AI free me for more meaningful work, or crowd out the parts I enjoy?
- Do I feel ownership of, and satisfaction in, what I produced?
- Next time, what will I hand to AI, and what will I keep for myself?

## Putting it into practice

You don't need to memorize anything. Before a task, pause to ask *why*. While you work, keep checking and keep thinking. Afterward, notice what changed. Over time, you'll learn where AI genuinely helps you, where it quietly costs you, and how to stay faithful to the truth and honest with yourself and others along the way.
